What does a textile sourcing agent in India charge?

By Vaishali Khairnar, Founder, Global Synctex Solutions · 25+ years in Indian textiles

Most sourcing agents charge in one of three ways: a commission on order value (typically 3–10%), a flat project fee, or a hybrid of the two. How an agent is paid shapes whose side they are on, so here is how each model works and what to watch for.

Buyers usually ask us two questions on the first call: “Which mill can make this?” and “What will you charge?” The second answer matters as much as the first.

1. Commission on order value

The agent takes a percentage of the order value. This is the most common model. Published industry guides put it at roughly 3–10% of order value, with higher rates on small first orders and lower rates on large repeat orders.

The commission should be calculated on the mill price (FOB or ex-mill), not on freight, duty or landed cost. Watch for: the agent earns more when you spend more, which can quietly favour pricier mills.

2. Flat or project fee

You pay a fixed amount for a defined job, such as shortlisting mills for one fabric, managing sampling, or running a pre-shipment inspection. Guides quote around USD 500–1,500 for a simple sourcing project and USD 3,000–10,000 for end-to-end project management. Check exactly what’s included: extra sampling rounds, lab tests or inspections may be billed separately.

3. Hybrid

A smaller commission (often 2–4%) plus fixed fees for specific services such as inspection or mill audits. Hybrids suit ongoing programmes, where you want the agent motivated on every order but don’t want the fee to grow just because the order does.

What the fee should cover

  • Matching your spec to suitable mills, with a shortlist showing each mill’s MOQ, price and lead time.
  • Swatches, lab dips and strike-offs.
  • Test reports against the methods you agree, such as ISO 811, ISO 4920 or ISO 12947-2.
  • Certificates: GRS for recycled content, OEKO-TEX, PFAS-free (C0) DWR confirmation, social-compliance audits.
  • Pre-shipment inspection at your AQL level.
  • Follow-up of bulk production through to shipment.

The hidden cost: commissions from the mill

Some agents also take an undisclosed commission from the mill, on top of what the buyer pays, so you never see the real mill price. Lawyers who advise importers call this an obvious conflict of interest and recommend contracts that require agents to disclose any payment from factories. Three simple protections:

  1. Ask the agent to name every mill and share the mill’s original quotation.
  2. Put in writing that the agent receives nothing from the mill, or discloses whatever it does receive.
  3. Define what the commission is calculated on, for example the FOB mill price only.

Agent, buying house or trading company?

  • A sourcing or buying agent works for you. The mill invoices and ships to you, and the agent charges a disclosed fee.
  • A trading company or merchant exporter buys from the mill and resells to you at a markup. That’s legitimate, but there’s no agent fee to compare, so ask how the price is built.
  • A buying house usually serves large retailers on long-term garment programmes. Some charge commission, others work on margin.

FAQ

Is a 5% commission normal?

Yes, for a mid-sized order. Published guides put most commissions between 3% and 10%, depending on order size and complexity.

Who pays the sourcing agent: the buyer or the mill?

A genuine buying agent is paid by the buyer. If the mill pays the agent, the agent is effectively selling for the mill, and that should be disclosed.

Are agent fees cheaper in India than in China?

Published ranges are similar across markets. Price differences come mostly from the mill, the construction and the order size, not from the agent's percentage.
